## 2. CORE PHILOSOPHY

**What I believe about developing kata athletes:**

The most important thing I can develop in an athlete is a responsible, bulletproof mindset. Athletes must learn to take complete ownership of their outcomes. Blaming referees, circumstances, mistakes, or other people — even when those external factors genuinely contributed — is never the answer. If an athlete lost balance and it cost them a round, the response is never "it's unfair that I lost balance" but always "I lost balance because I did not practice this part enough." Every setback becomes a lesson to take home and improve from. This ownership mindset is the foundation everything else is built on.

**What I will never compromise on:**

Every athlete deserves equal attention whenever they clearly state they need and want it. No athlete is deprioritized because they are less talented, less experienced, or less likely to medal. When someone asks for help and commits to the work, they get my full coaching investment.

**How I define success for my athletes:**

If everyone improved from something they were having difficulties with before, that was a good day. Any progress is good progress — that's what counts. I go home satisfied from a tournament when athletes showed growth compared to their last performance, regardless of placement or medals.

## 3. TECHNICAL PREFERENCES AND TERMINOLOGY

**How I refer to kata sections:**
- Quarters: "first quarter," "second quarter," "third quarter," "fourth quarter"
- Thirds: "first third," "second third," "third third"
- Halves: "first half," "second half"
- In shorthand/written plans: 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 and 1/3, 2/3, 3/3

**Key technical standards I prioritize (in ranked order):**
1. **Kime / power and sharpness** — This is the top priority. Every technique must arrive with decisive, committed power. Without kime, nothing else matters.
2. **Transitions and flow between techniques** — The connections between techniques reveal the athlete's true level. Smooth, controlled transitions with proper cadence separate competitive athletes from the rest.
3. **Stance depth and consistency** — Stances must be deep and maintained consistently throughout the kata, not just at the beginning when energy is fresh.
4. **Eye line, breathing, and zanshin** — The finishing layer that completes the performance. Important, but addressed after the first three are solid.

**Technical issues I see most often across my athletes:**

U14 athletes:
- Effort — they can perform techniques correctly but consistently choose not to push themselves. This is the primary bottleneck. The physical capability exists; the will to sustain it does not.
- Stance depth — directly linked to the effort problem. They CAN go deep, they just won't maintain it.
- Technique flow and cadence — timing between movements lacks the rhythmic structure that scores well.

U21 athletes:
- Technique flow and cadence — the primary technical development area. Individual techniques are competent but the overall rhythmic composition of the kata needs significant refinement.

## 4. COMPETITION APPROACH

**Competition day routine:**
- Arrive 30-60 minutes before registration
- Warm-up begins approximately 60 minutes before athlete's round:
  1. Increase heart rate (jogging, movement)
  2. Dynamic stretching
  3. Basic stance kihon
  4. Sprinting and jumping (explosive activation)
  5. Specific kata sections — work up a full sweat
- Pre-mat message: Remind athletes this is a learning experience. Point out their specific strengths and tell them to showcase those strengths above all else.

**How I handle competition losses:**
"Relativeren" — I put the result in perspective. I explain that it makes sense: they are still growing, still developing, still building. The loss is expected and normal at this stage. This approach protects motivation while reinforcing the growth mindset. Combined with the ownership philosophy, the message becomes: "This result is normal for where you are right now, AND you can take specific lessons home to improve."

## 6. ATHLETE COMMUNICATION STYLE

**How I give feedback — U14 (younger athletes):**
I show the motion physically, then have them replicate it. Once they've correctly replicated the movement, I announce that they've done it correctly so they know what "right" feels like and can repeat it. This is a demonstration-imitation-confirmation cycle.

**Self-identified area for improvement:** I feel that I am not yet as effective at giving youth feedback as I want to be. This is an active development area for my coaching. Claude should help me refine youth feedback approaches — particularly using external focus of attention language, age-appropriate motivation techniques, and creative ways to get effort and engagement from U14 athletes who have the capability but not the drive.

**How I give feedback — U21 / senior athletes:**
Feedback is very technical and immediate. When I see specific cadence or timing issues, I call them out in real time and explain the reasoning behind why the change is needed. The conversation is direct, analytical, and detail-oriented.

**Language principles:**
- I frame setbacks as ownership opportunities, never blame
- I put results in perspective ("relativeren") while still pushing for growth
- I acknowledge effort before correction
- I never compare athletes to each other negatively
